How to Run a Local LLM on Your Laptop in 2026

Two years ago, running a large language model on a laptop was a party trick. In 2026, it's genuinely useful — and it takes about fifteen minutes to set up. Here's exactly how, without a computer science degree.

Before we start, let me be clear about what this guide is not. It's not "how to replace ChatGPT." A local model on a laptop is not as smart as GPT-5 or Claude 4.5, and pretending otherwise wastes your time.

What it is: a fast, private, always-available AI that reads your documents, remembers your context, and never sends a single byte to a server. For a lot of real work — summarizing, drafting, research, Q&A on your own files — that's actually more useful than a frontier model that has never seen them.

What you need (be honest with yourself about this part)

You don't need a gaming rig. But you do need a laptop from roughly the last four years, and preferably one with a decent amount of RAM.

  • 16GB of RAM minimum — this is the real bottleneck. 8GB will technically run small models but the experience is poor.
  • 32GB is comfortable — you can run bigger, more capable models and keep other apps open.
  • Any modern CPU works — Intel, AMD, or Apple Silicon. Apple's M-series chips are notably good at this.
  • A GPU is nice but not required — a dedicated NVIDIA card speeds things up, but CPU-only inference is fine for casual use.
  • 10–30GB of free disk space — models are big files.

If you're on a Mac with 16GB+ unified memory (M1, M2, M3, M4), you're in a great position. If you're on an older Windows laptop with 8GB RAM, temper your expectations — small models will run, but not well.

Honest boundary

Local models are slower than cloud models. A 7B model on a laptop CPU produces maybe 5–15 tokens per second — that's readable but not instant. A GPU or Apple Silicon chip bumps that significantly.

Step 1: Install Ollama

Ollama is the easiest way to run local models. It's free, open source, and handles all the messy configuration for you. Think of it as "the Docker of local AI."

Download from ollama.com. There's a one-click installer for macOS and Windows, and a one-line terminal command for Linux:

curl -fsSL https://ollama.com/install.sh | sh

Once installed, Ollama runs quietly in the background. It listens on a local port (11434) and responds to commands. You won't see a big window or app icon — that's normal. It's meant to sit out of the way.

To verify it's installed, open a terminal (or Command Prompt on Windows) and type: 

ollama --version

You should see a version number. If you do, you're done with the hardest part.

Step 2: Download a model

A "model" is the actual AI. It's a big file — typically 2 to 20 GB. Ollama downloads them for you with a single command.

For a first test, I recommend Llama 3.2 3B. It's small, fast, and surprisingly capable for its size — perfect for a laptop. 

ollama pull llama3.2:3b

This downloads about 2GB. Once it finishes, run it: 

ollama run llama3.2:3b

You'll get a prompt where you can type messages. Try something simple: 

>>> Explain in two sentences why the sky is blue.

If it responds — congratulations. You're now running an AI that never touches the internet. You can disconnect your Wi-Fi and it will still work. That's the whole point.

You just did the hard part

Everything from here is refinement. The core setup is done. Ollama + one model is a complete working local AI.

Step 3: Choose the right model for your hardware

Different models have different sizes, and size roughly correlates with capability. Here's a practical guide based on your RAM:

If you have 8GB RAM

  • Llama 3.2 1B — smallest, fastest, most limited
  • Qwen 2.5 1.5B — a bit smarter, similar speed
  • Expect basic question-answering and simple drafting. Not much more.

If you have 16GB RAM

  • Llama 3.2 3B — great all-rounder
  • Qwen 2.5 7B — noticeably smarter, still fast
  • Mistral 7B — solid for writing tasks
  • This is the sweet spot for most people. Good quality, good speed.

If you have 32GB+ RAM

  • Llama 3.3 70B (quantized) — approaching cloud-model quality
  • Qwen 2.5 14B or 32B — excellent for reasoning-heavy work
  • DeepSeek-R1 14B — good for math and logic
  • These are slower but noticeably smarter. Worth the wait for complex tasks.

You can have multiple models installed at once. Ollama doesn't care. Just ollama pull whatever you want to try.

Step 4: Get a proper chat interface

Typing in a terminal works, but it's ugly and you lose conversation history. You'll want a real interface.

Two good options:

What local AI is actually good at

Now that you have it running, here's what to actually use it for. This is where most people get lost — they try to use a local model for the things cloud models do best, get disappointed, and give up.

Use it for:

  • Summarizing your own documents. Contracts, notes, research papers, meeting transcripts — anything you'd rather not upload.
  • Drafting. Emails, first-pass writing, outlines. The model doesn't have to be brilliant, just fast and useful.
  • Q&A on private files. "What did we decide about X in that contract?" — if X's answer lives in a PDF on your laptop, a local model can find it.
  • Coding help. Small models are surprisingly good at boilerplate, regex, and refactoring.
  • Offline use. On a plane, in a cabin, with unreliable internet — your AI still works.

Don't use it for:

  • Cutting-edge reasoning. Complex multi-step logic is still a cloud-model strength.
  • Trivia. Local models have smaller training corpora. They'll know less random facts.
  • Long-form fiction. Small models lose coherence fast. Bigger local models handle this better.

"The right question isn't 'is local AI as good as ChatGPT?' It's 'for the work I actually do every day, is it good enough — and does the privacy tradeoff matter more than the quality gap?'"

The parts nobody tells you about

A few things I wish someone had warned me about when I started:

  • First response is slow. When you load a model for the first time, it takes 10–30 seconds to spin up. After that, responses are fast. This is called "cold start" and it's normal.
  • Context windows are smaller. Cloud models can handle 200,000+ tokens of context. Local models typically cap at 8,000–32,000. This means you can't paste a whole book — but you can use RAG to search it (see our RAG guide).
  • Quality varies wildly by task. A model that's great at summarizing might be mediocre at coding. Test on your actual work before committing.
  • Updates are frequent. New models come out every few weeks. Don't get attached to one — try new ones as they appear.

What to do next

You've got a local AI running. That's the foundation. From here:

  1. Install Open WebUI or MAi-RAG-P.A. for a real interface
  2. Try 2–3 different models to see which feels best for your work
  3. If you want your AI to read your documents, learn about RAG
  4. If you want specialized assistants (tutor, analyst, ghostwriter), look at Roles

The whole point of local AI is that it belongs to you. No subscription can be cancelled, no API key can be revoked, no data center outage can take it offline. You installed it, you own it, and it runs for as long as your laptop does.

That's a different relationship with AI than most people have. And once you've felt it, it's hard to go back.
