The 10 Documents You Should Feed Your AI First

Installing a local AI is the easy part. The hard part is knowing what to actually put into it. Here are the ten documents worth ingesting first — and the exact questions you'll be able to ask once they're in.

When most people get a local AI running for the first time, they freeze. They stare at the upload button and think "what do I even put in here?"

So they test it with a few random PDFs, ask a couple of generic questions, and then lose interest — because the AI is answering from general knowledge, not from their content. It feels like a slightly worse version of ChatGPT.

The trick is choosing the right documents. Not the biggest pile. Not the most impressive-looking folder. Just the ten that actually change how useful the AI becomes.

Here they are, roughly in order of "should absolutely do this first."

1. Your contracts and agreements

Client contracts, vendor agreements, lease documents, employment paperwork, NDAs — anything with legal weight.

Why this first: contracts are exactly the kind of document you can't upload to a cloud AI. They contain confidential terms, named parties, pricing, and often restrictive clauses you'd rather not broadcast. A local AI lets you query them without any of that leaving your machine.

Once they're ingested, you can ask things like:

  • "What's the termination clause in the Client A contract?"
  • "Do any of my vendor agreements include auto-renewal language?"
  • "Which of my contracts have a non-compete clause?"
  • "Compare the payment terms across all my active agreements."

The AI will answer with citations to specific documents and pages, so you can verify everything. And unlike a lawyer, it's available at 11pm.

2. Your personal medical records and notes

Lab reports, doctor's notes, prescription histories, immunization records, insurance claims — whatever you have in digital form.

Why: this is the category that matters most, and it's the one people are most afraid to upload anywhere. Medical data is the single most sensitive category of personal information, and yet it's also the one where having AI help is genuinely valuable.

Once ingested, you can ask:

  • "What was my blood pressure at my last three visits?"
  • "Have any of my doctors mentioned a family history of X?"
  • "Which medications have I been on in the last five years?"
  • "Summarize my last specialist visit for a second opinion."

This is where local AI moves from "nice to have" to "actually important." Your health history should not be training data for someone else's product.

3. Your tax returns and financial statements

Last 3–5 years of tax returns, bank statements, investment summaries, mortgage documents, business financials — whatever applies to your situation.

Why: tax and financial documents are the second-most-sensitive category. They reveal your income, your debts, your assets, and often your family's entire financial picture. Financial advisors and accountants charge $200+/hour to answer questions about them.

Questions you can now ask:

  • "How has my effective tax rate changed over the last five years?"
  • "What deductions did I claim in 2023 that I didn't claim in 2024?"
  • "Summarize my mortgage terms for refinancing consideration."
  • "What's the trend in my business revenue based on the last 3 years of filings?"

4. Your meeting notes and call transcripts

If you're in a profession where you take notes during calls, meetings, or interviews, those notes are gold. Every hour of your week generates written context that vanishes when you close the document.

Why: meeting notes are the ultimate "I know I said this somewhere, but where?" category. They're also completely private — no client wants their call summarized by a cloud provider.

Examples:

  • "What did we decide about the pricing change in the October call?"
  • "What concerns did the client raise in our last three meetings?"
  • "Summarize every action item assigned to me in the last month."
  • "Find every time we discussed the Q4 timeline."

5. Your client briefs and project files

For freelancers, consultants, agencies — the working documents for each client. Style guides, brand guidelines, project specs, revision notes.

Why: this is where the "Clients A, B, and C" isolation matters. In MAi-RAG-PA, you can create a separate knowledge base for each client so their information never appears in another client's answers. You get the speed of having everything searchable without any cross-contamination.

Example workflow:

  • "What's Client A's preferred tone for technical content?"
  • "What revision cycle did Client B ask for?"
  • "Pull every deliverable spec from the Client C project folder."

6. Your insurance policies

Home, auto, health, life, business liability, umbrella — whatever policies you hold. These documents are long, jargon-heavy, and you rarely look at them until you need them urgently.

Why: insurance policies are written to be hard to read. They're 60-page PDFs with critical details buried in section 14.3(b). A local AI can find the answer to "am I covered for X?" in seconds, without uploading anything.

Questions you can ask:

  • "What's my deductible for water damage?"
  • "Does my policy cover damage from a power surge?"
  • "What's the process for filing a claim under my auto policy?"
  • "Compare coverage between my old and new home insurance policies."

7. Your family legal documents

Wills, trusts, powers of attorney, birth certificates, marriage licenses, property deeds, vehicle titles.

Why: these are the documents you need to access under stress — usually after a death, a move, a medical emergency, or a legal proceeding. Having them searchable means you don't have to dig through filing cabinets while dealing with everything else.

What you can ask:

  • "Who's listed as the executor of my will?"
  • "What does my power of attorney say about medical decisions?"
  • "Where is my property deed for the house on Oak Street?"

8. Your reading list and research papers

Academic papers, ebooks, articles you've saved for reference, white papers from your industry, technical documentation.

Why: this is where local AI becomes genuinely superior to cloud AI for certain tasks. If you're reading 50 papers on a subject, the AI can synthesize across all of them, cross-reference findings, and cite every claim. That's a workflow you can't really do in a web chatbot — and you definitely can't do it while keeping the papers private.

Example queries:

  • "Which of my saved papers discuss long-term outcomes of X?"
  • "Summarize the methodology differences between the 2023 and 2024 studies."
  • "Find every paper that mentions the effect of Y on Z."
  • "What are the main areas of disagreement in my research folder?"

9. Your own writing — drafts, journals, ideas

Unfinished essays, journal entries, idea fragments, character sketches, note-to-self documents, years of whatever you've been writing.

Why: this is the category most people overlook, and it's often the most valuable. Your own writing is the deepest record of your thinking — what you've already worked out, what you were circling around, what you were trying to say.

A local AI can help you rediscover that material. Ask things like:

  • "What did I write about loneliness in 2023?"
  • "Find every unfinished essay draft I started in the last two years."
  • "What ideas did I keep returning to but never developed?"
  • "Summarize the themes in my personal journal."

Some writers describe this as having a second self — someone who remembers everything you've ever written and can find any thread you've ever started.

10. Your company's policies and SOPs

Employee handbook, standard operating procedures, vendor lists, compliance docs, internal wikis, onboarding materials.

Why: if you run a business, this category saves more time than any other. Every time an employee asks a policy question, you either dig through folders or type out an answer. A local knowledge base ends that loop entirely.

Examples:

  • "What's our policy on remote work expenses?"
  • "Walk me through the procedure for handling a customer refund."
  • "What's the current onboarding checklist for new hires?"
  • "Which vendors are on our approved list for X category?"

If you have employees on the same local network, they can query the same knowledge base from their own computers — no cloud service, no per-seat license, no data leaving your building.

How to actually organize this (don't dump everything in one pile)

The single biggest mistake people make is uploading every document they own into one giant knowledge base. This produces worse results, because the AI has to search a huge haystack for every answer.

Instead, organize by purpose:

  • Personal legal — contracts, insurance, family docs
  • Medical — health records only
  • Financial — tax, bank, investment
  • Client A — one knowledge base per client
  • Client B — separate from A
  • Research — papers, ebooks, reference material
  • Writing — your own drafts and notes
  • Business operations — policies, SOPs, handbook

Each knowledge base stays small, focused, and fast. The AI finds answers faster, and — just as important — client data never leaks between bases. That's the Role-scoped isolation feature doing real work.

Start with five, not fifty

Don't try to ingest everything at once. Pick 5–10 documents from the categories above, ingest them, and use the AI for a week. You'll learn what questions you actually want to ask — and that tells you what to add next.

What NOT to ingest (yet)

A few categories seem appealing but produce disappointing results:

  • Your entire email archive. Too much noise, too little signal. Emails are usually better kept in your email client.
  • Random internet-saved PDFs. If you haven't read it, don't ingest it. Garbage in, garbage out.
  • Photos and scanned documents. Image-based PDFs need OCR first, which is a separate step. Skip until you have a specific need.
  • Your whole Google Drive. Same problem as email. Too broad. The AI gets lost.

The point isn't the documents

The reason these ten categories matter isn't that documents are inherently interesting. It's that each one represents a question you'd like answered but can't ask a cloud AI.

"What did my doctor say at my last visit?" Not asking that online. "What's the termination clause in my contract?" Not uploading that to a chatbot. "What did I write about grief two years ago?" Definitely not sending that to a company.

But all three questions have answers — sitting in documents on your drive, waiting for a tool that can read them without sending them anywhere.
