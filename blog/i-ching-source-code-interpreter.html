IV. The Code Was Always Binary

Five thousand years ago, Fuxi stood beside the Yellow River. Tradition says a dragon-horse emerged bearing the He Tu (河图), a pattern of dots and lines, a map of cosmic structure. A turtle from the Luo River bore the Luo Shu (洛书), completing the pair. From these, Fuxi derived the Eight Trigrams (八卦), and from the trigrams came everything else.

Centuries later, King Wen of Zhou (1152–1056 BCE) combined the trigrams into 64 hexagrams, six lines each, every line solid (yang = 1) or broken (yin = 0). Confucius added the Ten Wings. Commentators like Cheng Yi and Zhu Xi built interpretive frameworks on top. Richard Wilhelm carried the text West. Carl Jung wrote the foreword to his translation.

Strip away the commentary and the structure remains: 64 six-bit figures. Every line a 0 or a 1. The world's oldest binary code, and a native mechanism for state transition.

That last part is what almost everyone misses. The hexagrams are not static labels. The I Ching's architecture includes changing lines — positions where yin flips to yang or yang to yin, transforming one hexagram into another. The system doesn't just encode the current state of a process; it encodes the trajectory: this state, these transition points, that next state. In modern terms: the 64 hexagrams are the state-space, and the changing-line mechanism is the transition function. It is a finite-state machine describing the dynamics of change itself.

This is precisely what Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz recognized in 1703, when he encountered the hexagrams through Jesuit missionaries. The co-inventor of calculus, designer of the first machine capable of 16-digit calculation, saw in the I Ching a confirmation of his life's work: that all of reality could be expressed through the interplay of two symbols, that the universe was at its deepest level computational. His binary arithmetic, the direct ancestor of every processor on Earth, found its mirror in a book already ancient by his time.

Leibniz's encounter with the I Ching served as a powerful validation of his own independent discovery of binary arithmetic. In his 1703 paper Explication de l'Arithmétique Binaire, he explicitly wrote that he had found in the hexagrams a base for claiming the universality of the binary numeral system. By associating broken lines with 0 and solid lines with 1, he demonstrated that every one of the 64 hexagrams corresponded to a unique binary number in the range 000000–111111 (0–63 in decimal notation).

He wasn't projecting. He was reading the code correctly.

VI. The Cosmic Clock: Shao Yong and the Decoding of Time Itself

Most readers of the I Ching encounter only its spatial dimension — the structure of a situation. But the Daoist tradition contains a second, far more ambitious system: a temporal decoder.

The Song dynasty philosopher Shao Yong (邵雍, 1011–1077) took the binary logic of the I Ching to its logical extreme in the Huangji Jingshi (皇极经世, "August Ultimate through the Ages"). If the hexagrams describe the phases of all cycles, then time itself is a nested set of cycles, and if cycles are deterministic, the allocation of hexagrams to epochs can be computed. Not divined. Computed.

Shao Yong was one of the most diversely talented neo-Confucians of 11th-century China — a cosmologist, historian, poet, and ethicist whose Treatise of Supreme World-Ordering Principles harmonized Daoist ontology, Buddhist cosmology, and Confucian ethics into a single comprehensive system. His Huangji Jingshi presents a cycle of the universe totaling 129,600 years, subdivided into nested temporal scales.

Shao Yong built the cosmic clock. Time is allocated hierarchically:

  • Yuan (元) — the epoch, the great cycle
  • Hui (会) — the age within the epoch
  • Yun (运) — the revolution within the age
  • Shi (世) — the generation within the revolution
  • Nian (年) — the year within the generation

Each level receives its hexagram allocation across the two canonical sequences: the Fu Xi order (the primordial binary structure) and the King Wen order (the manifested, situational reality). Read together, they describe the onto-cosmological weather of any moment in history — the phase of the great loop that the world currently occupies.

The hexagram-allocated table exhaustively exhibits the 129,600 year lines of allocated hexagrams correlated with four Pillars (Yuan, Hui, Yun, Shi) as well as 4 Emblems, 24 solar terms, and 60 on-duty hexagrammatic elements. The whole database elucidates a self-consistent, hierarchical, and nested temporal structure of the solar-terrestrial system.
